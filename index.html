Jiayi Zhang

I study cultural sociology, focusing on how people make sense of the past and, through it, of themselves, their communities, and the world, with China as my main case. Combining computational text analysis with close reading and fieldwork, I study where official narratives of Chinese history meet alternative public beliefs in film, television, literature, and online commentary. I ask what individual and collective values emerge from these encounters, and what this ongoing process of meaning-making reveals about how social order changes and how it is maintained. I am an M.A. candidate in East Asian Studies at the University of California, Los Angeles, where I am also completing a graduate certificate in Digital Humanities.

Much of my research asks how audiences remember China’s twentieth-century upheavals. In an article with Chandler Rosenberger in the International Journal of Politics, Culture, and Society (2026), we use seeded topic modeling on Douban reviews of fifth-generation films to map the distinct ways viewers make sense of the past without official guidance. Rosenberger, a sociologist at Brandeis who studies how culture shapes perception and politics, has been my mentor since 2022. My current paper extends this work to The Blue Kite, a banned film, asking how a community of viewers builds shared meaning around a past that official channels withhold. With Rosenberger, I am also studying the contested afterlife of the writer Lu Xun, drawing on my fieldwork at the Shanghai Lu Xun Museum.

My M.A. thesis, Alternative Histories, Forbidden Futures: Time Travel Narratives and the Politics of Historical Imagination in Chinese Popular Culture, examines how Chinese television imagines the Qing past, centering on the time-travel drama Scarlet Heart and its relationship to Yongzheng Dynasty. Pairing close reading of the dramas with computational analysis of thousands of Douban comments and reviews, I ask how viewers move between personal and national pasts, and where their historical imagination meets its limits.

I have taught computational text analysis as a teaching assistant for Digital Humanities 120 at UCLA, and co-designed and co-taught Cognitive Sociology with Rosenberger at Brandeis University, where I earned my B.A. in Psychology, Philosophy, and International and Global Studies.
